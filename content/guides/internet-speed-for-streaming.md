Streaming services adapt quality to your connection, so a slow line doesn't stop playback; it quietly lowers picture quality. Here's how to make sure you get the 4K you're paying for.

## Speed per stream

Netflix publishes these recommendations, and other services are in the same range:

| Quality | Recommended speed |
| --- | --- |
| HD (720p) | 3 Mbps or higher |
| Full HD (1080p) | 5 Mbps or higher |
| Ultra HD (4K) | 15 Mbps or higher |

These are **per stream**. A household watching two 4K streams and one HD stream needs at least 35 Mbps for video alone.

## Size the plan for your busiest evening

Add up everything that might run at once: TV streams, video calls (about 4 Mbps each, both directions), online and cloud gaming, smart cameras and speakers, phones and laptops. Then add **about 50% headroom** for Wi-Fi overhead and spikes. The [bandwidth planner](bandwidth.html) does this with steppers for each activity.

Rough guide:

| Household | Comfortable plan |
| --- | --- |
| 1–2 people, mostly HD | 100 Mbps |
| Family, 1–2 4K streams + calls | 300 Mbps |
| Busy family, several 4K streams, gaming, work from home | 500 Mbps–1 Gbps |

## Don't ignore upload

Download gets the headlines, but **upload** matters for video calls, cloud backup, streaming your own media server remotely and live-streaming games. Cable plans often have far less upload than download; fiber is usually symmetrical. If you work from home or run a Plex/Jellyfin server for family elsewhere, prioritise upload.

## Data caps

Streaming uses roughly **7 GB per hour in 4K** and **3 GB per hour in HD**. Four hours of 4K a day is about 840 GB a month from viewing alone. Some providers cap monthly data (1.2 TB is a common cable cap) and charge overage. If you're close, look for an unlimited plan.

## Buffering? Check Wi-Fi before upgrading

Most buffering happens between the router and the TV, not between your home and the internet.

1. **Wire the main TV or streamer** with Ethernet if at all possible. It removes Wi-Fi from the equation.
2. **Move the router** to a central, raised, open spot, not in a cabinet or basement corner.
3. **Use mesh Wi-Fi** in larger or multi-floor homes so the TV connects to a nearby node.
4. **Use modern Wi-Fi.** Wi-Fi 6, 6E or 7 handles many devices far better than older standards.
5. **Test at the TV,** not on your phone next to the router.

## Media servers and the home network

If you run a home media server, playback inside the house uses your local network, not your internet plan. A wired gigabit connection to the server and main TV lets even high-bitrate 4K files play without transcoding. See [how to build a home media server](guides/build-a-home-media-server.html).
