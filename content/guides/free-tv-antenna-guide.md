An antenna is the cheapest upgrade in home entertainment: a one-time purchase that brings in local news, network shows and many major sports events in HD, with no monthly bill. Here's how to make it work.

## 1. Check reception at your address

Signal strength depends on distance to the broadcast towers, terrain and buildings. Before you buy, use a reception-map tool for your address (in the US, the FCC publishes reception maps; independent tools such as RabbitEars and antenna makers' channel finders also exist). They show which channels you can expect, how strong they are, and **which direction** the towers are.

## 2. Pick the right antenna type

| Situation | Antenna |
| --- | --- |
| Close to towers (roughly under 25 miles), good signal | Indoor flat or compact antenna |
| Medium distance or towers in different directions | Attic or outdoor multi-directional antenna |
| Long distance or weak signals | Outdoor directional antenna, mounted high |

Important detail: some stations broadcast on **VHF** frequencies and many on **UHF**. If your channel list includes VHF stations, choose an antenna rated for both. Ignore "200-mile" marketing claims; terrain and the curvature of the earth limit real range.

## 3. Placement is everything

- **Higher is better.** An attic or roof beats a TV stand.
- **Face the towers.** Use the direction from the reception map.
- **Windows help.** Building materials like brick, concrete and metal siding block signal.
- **Rescan after every move.** The TV only finds channels during a scan.

Try a few positions and rescan each time; a few feet can make the difference between 10 and 40 channels.

## 4. Amplifiers and splitters

An amplifier compensates for **long cable runs** and **splitting** to several TVs. It doesn't create signal that isn't there, and near strong towers it can overload the tuner. Start without one; add it only if you're splitting or running long cable.

## 5. Record and stream antenna TV

An **OTA DVR** connects to the antenna and records shows to a hard drive or cloud, then streams live and recorded channels to TVs, phones and tablets on your network. Some media-server software (Plex, Jellyfin, Emby) can also act as a DVR with a compatible network tuner.

## 6. NEXTGEN TV (ATSC 3.0)

In many US markets, stations also broadcast in **ATSC 3.0**, marketed as NEXTGEN TV. It can carry 4K, HDR and improved audio where stations offer it, but needs a compatible TV or tuner. Standard HD broadcasts continue alongside it in most markets for now.

## How much does it save?

Local channels are often what keeps people paying for a cable TV package. With an antenna covering them, many households drop the expensive live-TV tier and keep only one or two on-demand services.
