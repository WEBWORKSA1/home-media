TVs leave the factory set to stand out under bright shop lights: blue-tinted whites, over-sharpened edges and motion smoothing. At home, that look is tiring and inaccurate. Ten minutes in the menus fixes most of it.

## Step 1: Pick the right picture mode

Choose, in order of preference:

1. **Filmmaker Mode** (where available)
2. **Movie**, **Cinema** or **Calibrated** mode
3. **Standard** as a last resort

Avoid **Vivid** or **Dynamic**. These modes crush detail in bright areas and push colours far beyond what was mastered. HDR content usually switches to its own mode set; repeat the same choice there (e.g. "Filmmaker Mode HDR" or "Movie HDR").

## Step 2: Turn off motion smoothing for films

Motion interpolation inserts invented frames to make motion smoother. On films shot at 24 frames per second it creates the "soap opera effect". Look for settings called TruMotion, Motionflow, Auto Motion Plus, Action Smoothing or similar, and set them to **Off** for movies. For sport, a low setting can reduce blur; that's personal taste.

## Step 3: Set white balance and colour

- **Colour temperature:** Warm, Warm 2 or "Expert" warm. Cool and Normal make whites blue.
- **Colour:** leave at the mode's default unless faces look obviously wrong.
- **Turn off** "dynamic colour", "colour boost", "live colour" and similar enhancers.

## Step 4: Brightness for your room

Two different controls are often confused:

- **Backlight / OLED light / Brightness (on some brands)** sets how bright the whole picture is. Raise it in a bright room, lower it at night.
- **Black level / Brightness (on others)** sets where black sits. If you can't see detail in dark clothing, it's too low; if blacks look grey, it's too high. Most of the time, the mode default is right.

## Step 5: Sharpness and noise reduction

Set **sharpness** to zero or the mode's neutral value (some TVs treat 0 as neutral, others use a mid-point). Over-sharpening adds halos. Turn **noise reduction** off for 4K and Blu-ray; a low setting can help old or low-bitrate streams.

## Step 6: HDR settings

- Enable the **enhanced HDMI** or "Input signal plus" option on the input your 4K sources use, or you may not get HDR or 4K at 60/120 Hz. Check what your connection needs with the [HDMI checker](hdmi.html).
- Use **dynamic tone mapping** if your TV has it and highlights look clipped; it's one processing feature that usually helps.
- If your TV has an ambient light sensor that dims HDR in the evening and you find it too dark, turn it off.

## Gaming

Use **Game mode** (or set the input to "PC/Game") for the lowest input lag, and enable **VRR** and **ALLM** if your console supports them. You can still apply warm colour temperature in game mode.

## When to call a professional

A professional calibrator uses a colour meter and test patterns to set white balance and colour gamut precisely, often with an audio calibration too. It's worth it for projectors (whose colour shifts with bulb or laser age and screen material), high-end displays in dedicated rooms, and anyone who's simply particular.
